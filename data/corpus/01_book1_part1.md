# The Long Patience, Part One

## Chapter 1 — The Tide Clock

Halvern Reach begins where the basalt ends. The city is cut back into the cliff in seven terraces,
and on the lowest of them, in a hall open to the weather on three sides, stands the Tide Clock.

The Clock is a water clock with four dials. The first dial counts the tides, which at the Reach
run twice in a day and a little over. The second counts the hours, in the Reach's own reckoning of
twenty hours to the day. The third is the dial of the Line: a slow brass ring, one turn to the
year, marked at forty points, which tells a caravan master how long the light will hold at each
waystone in the season he means to travel. The fourth dial is set behind a brass plate that has
never, in the Clock's recorded keeping, been opened. The Keeper is permitted to wind it. The Keeper
is not told what it counts.

In the winter of Hollow 1143 the Keeper of the Tide Clock was **Ilsa Mirren**, a cartographer by
training, who had held the office eleven years and had the Reach's only complete survey of the
waystones hanging in rolls behind her desk.

"Forty stones," she said, when Pell Varrow asked her, as every courier eventually asks. "Forty, from
the one in the Syndic's courtyard to the one in the Glass Bazaar at Kesh Ithar. Who set them there,
nobody knows. Not the Syndicate, not the Tables, not the Guild. They were here before the Concord
and there is no record older than the Concord. People who tell you otherwise are selling something."

## Chapter 2 — Fourteen Wagons

The caravan *Long Patience* left Halvern Reach in Hollow of 1143 with **fourteen wagons**.

Its master was **Oren Dask**, fifty-eight years old that winter, who had spent his first career as
a clerk in the Harbour Syndicate's tithe office and his second undoing, wagon by wagon, the harm he
felt he had done in the first. He knew the tithe schedule the way other men know a prayer. He had
written parts of it.

Dask kept three rules, and said them at the start of every passage so that nobody could claim not
to have heard:

1. The wagons move at the pace of the slowest wheel.
2. Nobody drinks at a stone the Compact holds until the toll is settled.
3. The outrider's word on the road is the master's word.

The outrider of the *Long Patience* was **Sera Volant**, twenty-four years old, born at Varro's
Ford in Thaw of 1119. She had joined the caravan in Greening of 1141 and had ridden two full
passages before this one. She wore at her belt a brass astrolabe that had been her mother's, worn
smooth at the limb from handling, with a hairline crack across the alidade that she had never had
repaired because the repair would have taken the scratches with it.

With them travelled **Pell Varrow**, sixteen, a courier of the Reach, apprenticed to Sera for the
passage at the Syndic's expense and against the Syndic's better judgement.

## Chapter 3 — The Syndic's Courtyard

**Halbrecht Oye**, Harbour Syndic of Halvern Reach, came down to the courtyard to see the caravan
counted. This was not courtesy. The first waystone of the Line stands in his courtyard, and the
Waystone Tithe is reckoned from the count taken beneath it.

"Fourteen," said the tithe clerk.

"Fourteen," Dask agreed.

"The whole Line?"

"To the Bazaar."

The clerk did the arithmetic that clerks of the Reach do in their heads and wrote the figure in the
book, and Oye signed beneath it, and that was the Reach's claim on the *Long Patience* for the
season. Dask did not look at the figure. He had spent eleven years writing figures in that book and
he knew what the schedule made of fourteen wagons and forty stones.

"You disapprove of the boy," he said instead.

"I disapprove of couriers who want to be outriders," said Oye. "The Reach has sixty couriers and
four roads. It has one Line."

Halvern Reach held forty-one thousand people that year. It had held more, before the Flats widened.

## Chapter 4 — To the Ford

The Line runs south from the Reach along the shoulder of the cliffs for the first six stones, and
the going is easy and nobody is grateful for it, because the Flats begin at the twelfth.

At the ninth stone the Line meets the Vessel River, and there is exactly one place in the river's
whole length where a loaded wagon can cross it: a shelf of flat rock a little under two feet below
the water in an ordinary season. The town that grew on the shelf is called **Varro's Ford**, and in
1143 two thousand three hundred people lived in it, most of them in the business of getting other
people's axles out of the water.

Sera rode ahead to the Ford, as the outrider does, and came back and said the shelf was clear and
the water was low, and did not say anything else about the Ford, and Dask did not ask her to. He
knew what the Ford was to her. Her mother, **Ravenna Volant**, had been a cartographer, and had
drowned in the Vessel when it came up in Emberwane of 1131, surveying the one crossing on the Line
that the Syndicate had never been able to tax, because you cannot put a tithe clerk on a rock that
is underwater four months in eight.

Sera had been twelve.

"The stones at the Ford are the oldest-looking of any on the Line," Pell said, that evening, in the
tone of someone repeating something. "My grandmother says they were cut by the people who dug the
Flats."

"Nobody dug the Flats," said Sera. "And nobody knows who cut the stones. Those are two different
kinds of not knowing and it's worth keeping them apart."

The *Long Patience* crossed the Vessel at the ninth stone and went south toward the twelfth, where
the grass gives up.
