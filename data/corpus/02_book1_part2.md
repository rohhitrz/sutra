# The Long Patience, Part Two

## Chapter 5 — The Fourteenth Stone

The Ashen Flats begin at the twelfth stone and end at the twenty-fourth, and in between there is
no water that a caravan has not carried in or paid for.

There is, in principle, a way around. At the fourteenth stone a track leaves the Line eastward into
the hills and climbs through the **Thennic Spur**, and comes down again at the twenty-second stone,
cutting out eight stones of salt. Caravans used it for three centuries.

In Frostmark of 1139 the shoulder above the narrows came down and filled the pass for a hundred
yards with rock the size of wagons, and the Thennic Spur has been closed ever since. The Lamplighters
let the signal fires at the two ends go out, which is the Guild's way of saying a route is shut, and
in 1143 they had not relit them.

Dask looked up the track anyway, as he did every passage, the way a man checks a door he knows is
locked.

"It would save us twelve days," said Pell.

"It would save us twelve days and eight stones of tithe," said Dask, "and the Guild would have my
licence for it, and they would be right to. The fires are out. When the Guild lights them, the road
is a road. Until then it's a rockfall with a name."

## Chapter 6 — Ossuary Gate

The *Long Patience* came to the seventeenth stone in Dustfall of 1143.

The seventeenth stone stands under a broken arch of the same stone as the waystones themselves,
and the arch is called **Ossuary Gate**, though there are no bones in it and never have been. It
is the exact middle of the Line: seventeen stones is not the middle of forty, but the Line is not
evenly spaced, and the Gate is the middle by distance and by water, which are the only two measures
a caravan cares about.

Nobody lives at Ossuary Gate. There is a cistern, a wind-break, a Lamplighters' fire-pan on a
pole, and a tender.

The tender was **Hesh**, who had no voice and had never had one, and who kept the Gate's records in
knotted cord — a yard of cord for each season, the knots counting wagons, the spacing counting days,
a double knot for a caravan that arrived short of what it had left with. The Guild had tried twice
to replace the cords with a ledger and had twice been unable to explain to a man who could not be
spoken to why the ledger was better. Hesh's cords went back thirty-one seasons and had never once
been found wrong.

Hesh counted the fourteen wagons, tied fourteen knots, and gave Dask water.

## Chapter 7 — The Water Toll

In Emberwane of 1143 the **Ashen Compact** closed the cisterns from the eighteenth stone to the
twenty-second and set a price on them.

The Compact are the people of the Flats, which is a sentence that does a great deal of work. They
are not bandits, whatever the Reach's broadsheets say; they are perhaps four thousand people living
in a place where the only wealth is water and the only traffic is other people's. Their arrangement
is simple and has not changed in living memory: the Line crosses their ground, the cisterns on their
ground are theirs, and a caravan that wants to drink pays a **water toll**, in silver or in kind, at
a rate set each season by the Compact's own reckoning and not negotiable at the cistern's edge.

Their leader in 1143 was **Marrowen Vehl**, called the Cartwright, a broad grey man of about fifty
who had in fact been a cartwright, and who conducted the Compact's business from the back of a
wagon he had built himself and would not let anyone else drive.

The toll that season was high. Dask paid it at the eighteenth and the nineteenth and the twentieth.

## Chapter 8 — The Standoff at the Twenty-First

At the twenty-first stone, Dask stopped paying.

What followed took six days and is remembered at the Reach as a robbery and in the Flats as a
negotiation, and was in truth neither. Two hundred and forty people stood on opposite sides of a
cistern in Emberwane heat while two men who both understood arithmetic very well indeed waited for
the other to be the first to want something badly enough to say so.

Four of the Compact's people were hurt on the third day, and two of the caravan's, and one of the
caravan's — a drover named Attick Sell, who had been with Dask nine years — died of it on the
fifth. The Compact's healer sat with him. That detail is in no broadsheet at the Reach.

On the sixth day Sera Volant rode out alone to Vehl's wagon, without Dask's leave, carrying her
mother's astrolabe, and came back four hours later with the cisterns open and the toll settled at
rather less than the Compact had asked and rather more than Dask had offered.

She would not say what had been said. Dask, who had three rules and had just watched the third one
used against him, did not press her.

"He knew the astrolabe," was all she said, and Dask took it for the sort of thing travellers say
about Flats-folk, and let it alone. He was wrong to.

## Chapter 9 — The Glass Bazaar

The *Long Patience* came down out of the Flats at the twenty-fourth stone and reached **Kesh Ithar**
in Frostmark of 1143, with thirteen of its fourteen wagons still carrying and the fourteenth
carrying Attick Sell.

Kesh Ithar is an oasis that became a city and has never quite forgiven itself. Sixty-eight thousand
people live in it. The fortieth and last waystone of the Line stands in the middle of the Glass
Bazaar with a market built up against it on all four sides, so that it has become, in practice, a
pillar holding up somebody's awning.

The city is governed by the **Nine Tables**, nine merchant seats, each with one vote and each held
for life. The fourth seat in 1143 belonged to **Tulvo Brann**, who dealt in glass, salt, brass and
whatever else moved, and who kept the most complete private ledger of Line traffic in either city —
a fact the Harbour Syndicate found irritating and useful in roughly equal measure.

Brann bought the *Long Patience*'s glass at a good price and its salt at a better one, and asked
Dask three careful questions about the standoff at the twenty-first stone, and wrote nothing down
while Dask answered, which Dask noticed.

Book One ends there: in Frostmark of 1143, in the Glass Bazaar, with the accounts settled, the
cords at the Gate tied, and the Thennic Spur still shut.
